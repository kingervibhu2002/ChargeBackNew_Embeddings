# CVV/CVC Card Verification

## What Is CVV/CVC?

CVV (Card Verification Value) and CVC (Card Validation Code) are security codes used by payment card networks to verify that a card is physically present or that the cardholder has access to the card's printed data. Different networks use different terminology for essentially the same concept:

- **Visa:** CVV (Card Verification Value) and CVV2
- **Mastercard:** CVC (Card Validation Code) and CVC2
- **American Express:** CID (Card Identification Number, 4 digits on the front)
- **Discover:** CVD (Card Validation Data)

Despite the different names, the verification mechanism works the same way across all major networks. This document uses "CVV" as the generic term for all variants.

## The Four Types of CVV

Understanding the different CVV types is important for both fraud prevention and chargeback evidence:

### CVV1 — Magnetic Stripe CVV
CVV1 is encoded in Track 2 of the magnetic stripe. It is different from the printed CVV2 number. CVV1 is used in card-present swipe transactions to verify that the physical card's magnetic stripe is authentic (not a counterfeit). CVV1 is never visible to the cardholder or merchant; it is transmitted automatically during the swipe. If a counterfeit magnetic stripe card is created from stolen Track 2 data, the CVV1 will match (which is why chip cards were introduced — the chip generates a different verification method that cannot be cloned).

### CVV2 — Printed CNP Code
CVV2 is the 3-digit code printed on the signature panel on the back of Visa and Mastercard cards (or the 4-digit code printed on the front of Amex cards). CVV2 is specifically designed for card-not-present transactions. When a customer enters their CVV2 at checkout, they are demonstrating that they have physical access to the card (or at least to the card's printed data).

CVV2 is never stored in databases or encoded on the magnetic stripe — it exists only on the physical card. This means that breached card databases (obtained from point-of-sale system hacks or processor databases) typically do not include CVV2, because it is never stored. Requiring CVV2 at checkout filters out many forms of card data theft.

### CVV3 — Chip Transaction Dynamic Value
For EMV chip transactions, a dynamic transaction-specific authentication value is generated by the chip for each transaction. This is sometimes called CVV3 or dCVV. It cannot be reused or cloned, which is why chip card fraud is rare at chip-enabled terminals. Not applicable to CNP transactions.

### iCVV — Dynamic CVV (Contactless/NFC)
iCVV is used in contactless (tap-to-pay) transactions. Like CVV3, it is dynamically generated per transaction and cannot be replayed. The value differs from CVV1, so a counterfeit magnetic stripe card cannot be used as a contactless card using captured iCVV data.

## How CVV Is Verified at Authorization

When a customer enters their CVV2 at checkout, the value is transmitted to the issuing bank as part of the authorization request — along with the card number, expiry date, amount, and AVS data. The issuer compares the provided CVV2 against its internal record (issuers store the CVV2 value themselves; it is not stored in merchant systems) and returns a CVV response code.

The CVV check occurs in real time during the authorization. The result is returned alongside the authorization approval or decline code.

## CVV Response Codes

| Code | Meaning | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| M | Match — CVV matches issuer record | Positive indicator; cardholder has card data |
| N | No Match — CVV does not match | High fraud risk; consider declining |
| P | Not Processed — CVV not sent or not processed | Neutral; re-submit with CVV if possible |
| S | CVV should be on card but issuer indicated it is not | Unusual; treat as risk signal |
| U | Issuer not participating in CVV | Cannot verify; use other controls |
| Blank/Null | No CVV check performed | Check your integration |

**M (Match):** The most expected result for a legitimate transaction where the cardholder entered their CVV2. An M code indicates the CVV2 provided matches the issuer's record, meaning the customer had access to the printed number.

**N (No Match):** A fraudster using stolen card data without CVV2, or who entered an incorrect CVV2, produces an N. Most merchants should decline N-coded transactions or require manual review. The fraud rate on CVV N transactions is significantly elevated.

**P (Not Processed):** CVV was not sent in the authorization request, or the issuer did not process it. Check your payment integration to ensure CVV2 is being collected and transmitted on all transactions. Not transmitting CVV2 is a compliance and fraud prevention failure.

## Using CVV as Chargeback Evidence

CVV verification data from the authorization record is included in your chargeback evidence package as proof that:
1. You required CVV verification at checkout (demonstrating fraud screening practices).
2. The CVV matched the issuer's record (indicating the transaction presenter had access to the card's printed verification data).

**How to present CVV evidence:**
- Pull the authorization response from your gateway or processor portal.
- Locate the CVV response code (often labeled "CVV Result," "CVC Result," or "Card Security Code Result").
- State in your rebuttal letter: "The card verification value (CVV2) provided by the customer matched the issuer's records (CVV response: M), indicating the individual had physical access to the card's printed security code."
- Combine with AVS result for a stronger authorization evidence package.

**Important limitation:** CVV match proves only that the presenter knew the CVV2 value. It does not prove the presenter was the legitimate cardholder. A fraudster who purchased complete card data — including CVV2 — from a dark web market (where some sellers include CVV data) will produce an M code just as a legitimate cardholder would. This is why CVV alone is insufficient as a chargeback defense.

## PCI DSS Prohibition on Storing CVV

This is a critical compliance requirement: PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) **absolutely prohibits** merchants from storing CVV2 data after authorization is complete. This means:

- You cannot store the CVV2 in your order database.
- You cannot log the CVV2 in your transaction logs.
- You cannot save the CVV2 in your CRM system.
- You cannot include the CVV2 in receipts (electronic or paper).

You CAN store the CVV response code (M, N, P) — the result of the verification — but not the actual CVV value itself.

Merchants found storing CVV2 face significant PCI DSS penalties including fines from payment networks, mandatory forensic audits, and potential termination of card acceptance privileges. If you are uncertain whether your systems store CVV2, conduct an immediate audit with your payment system vendor.

For recurring billing, you NEVER re-check CVV on subsequent charges after the initial transaction — which is why the initial CVV match is particularly important to document.

## Limitations of CVV

### CVV Proves Card Data Access, Not Cardholder Identity

CVV verification narrows the fraud vector — it filters out fraudsters who only have card numbers without CVV — but it does not prove the person entering the CVV is the legitimate cardholder. Any fraudster who has purchased a "fullz" (complete cardholder data package including CVV) from a criminal marketplace will pass CVV verification.

### CVV Cannot Be Re-Checked for Recurring Billing

Once a subscription is set up, subsequent recurring charges cannot include CVV verification (the customer is not present to enter it, and you cannot store the CVV from enrollment). The initial CVV check at subscription enrollment is therefore the only CVV verification opportunity for the entire subscription lifecycle. Document and retain this initial result carefully.

### International Cards and CVV Availability

Some issuing banks — particularly in certain international markets — do not participate in CVV verification (response code U) or have cards without CVV2 (response code S). This does not make the transaction fraudulent, but it means CVV cannot be used as a fraud signal or evidence for those transactions.

### Not All Card Types Support CVV2

Certain prepaid cards, virtual cards, and corporate cards may not have CVV2 values or may not participate in CVV verification. Treat U or S responses from these card types with appropriate risk-based judgment rather than automatic decline.

## Summary

CVV2 verification is a fundamental CNP fraud control that narrows the fraud vector by requiring knowledge of the card's printed security code — information not typically included in point-of-sale data breaches. An M (Match) response is a positive fraud signal and useful supporting evidence in chargeback rebuttals, demonstrating the customer had access to card security data at time of purchase. However, CVV is never a complete defense on its own, and storing CVV2 post-authorization is a serious PCI DSS violation. Use CVV as one layer in a multi-factor fraud control stack alongside AVS, 3DS, device fingerprinting, and fraud scoring.
