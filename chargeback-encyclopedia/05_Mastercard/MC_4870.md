# Mastercard 4870 — Chip Liability Shift

## What This Reason Code Means

Mastercard reason code 4870 is a counterfeit card fraud chargeback that occurs specifically because the merchant processed a chip-enabled card by reading the magnetic stripe instead of the EMV chip. When a chip card is swiped rather than dipped, the EMV liability shift rules reassign financial responsibility for counterfeit fraud from the card issuer to the merchant.

Before the EMV liability shift (which Mastercard implemented in the United States in October 2015), card issuers bore the cost of counterfeit card fraud as a cost of doing business. After the liability shift, any merchant who could have read a chip but chose to swipe became responsible for counterfeit fraud losses at their terminal.

This reason code represents one of the clearest examples in the chargeback system where merchant liability is determined entirely by infrastructure — not by the quality of evidence submitted at time of dispute.

---

## How Counterfeit Card Fraud Works

Counterfeit card fraud involves criminals duplicating the data stored on a payment card and encoding it onto a blank card — typically called a "cloned card." The criminal uses this cloned card at point-of-sale terminals to make fraudulent purchases.

The magnetic stripe on payment cards encodes static data — the same cardholder number, expiration date, and service code every time. This static data is relatively easy to skim and copy using a skimming device placed on ATMs or terminals.

**EMV chips are different.** Chips generate a unique, one-time cryptogram for each transaction using the card's private key. Even if a criminal intercepts the data from a chip transaction, the cryptogram cannot be reused — making chip transaction data effectively worthless for counterfeiting.

When a chip card is swiped rather than inserted, the magnetic stripe data is used — and that data can be counterfeited. The liability shift exists to create a financial incentive for merchants to upgrade terminals and eliminate swipe-only processing.

---

## The Liability Shift Rule

Under Mastercard's EMV liability shift:

- If a merchant's terminal **can read a chip** and the transaction is completed via chip, the **issuer bears the fraud liability** (as before 2015)
- If a merchant's terminal **cannot read a chip** (swipe-only) and the card has a chip, the **merchant bears the fraud liability**
- If the card itself has no chip (magnetic-stripe-only card), the liability shift does not apply — the issuer remains responsible

This rule is straightforward: equip your terminal to read chips, and you are protected. Use a swipe-only terminal with a chip card, and you absorb counterfeit losses.

## Chip Data vs. Magnetic Stripe Data in Authorization Records

Every payment authorization record contains a field called the **Point of Service Entry Mode (POS Entry Mode)** — a numeric code that indicates how card data was captured:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 05 | Contact chip read (EMV) |
| 07 | Contactless chip (tap-to-pay, NFC) |
| 01 | Manual key-entry |
| 90 | Magnetic stripe read |
| 91 | Contactless magnetic stripe (swipe equivalent) |

When a 4870 chargeback is filed, the issuer checks this field. If the code is **90** (stripe read) on a chip-capable card, the liability shifts to the merchant automatically. If the code is **05** or **07** (chip read), the chargeback cannot stand under 4870 because the merchant used the secure method.

This is why the evidence for a 4870 defense is not a document you write — it is data embedded in the original authorization record. You need to obtain that authorization record from your acquirer and confirm whether your terminal captured chip or stripe data.

---

## Evidence for a 4870 Defense

**If your terminal read the chip:**

The authorization record will contain POS Entry Mode 05 or 07, along with an EMV Application Cryptogram (AC) — a string of data generated by the chip during the transaction. This cryptogram is unforgeable proof that the genuine chip was present and read. Submit:

- Full authorization record from your acquirer showing POS Entry Mode 05 or 07
- EMV cryptogram data from the authorization record
- Terminal configuration report confirming chip capability is enabled

With this evidence, the 4870 chargeback should be reversed.

**If your terminal swiped instead of reading the chip:**

You almost certainly cannot win this dispute. The liability shift applies unconditionally. Do not waste resources constructing an elaborate rebuttal — accept the chargeback and prioritize terminal upgrade.

## Why Merchants Must Upgrade Terminals

Merchants with swipe-only terminals face an asymmetric risk profile: every counterfeit card transaction at their terminal is their financial loss. Chip-capable terminals cost between $200 and $600 depending on model and features — far less than the cumulative losses from even a modest volume of counterfeit fraud chargebacks.

Beyond financial loss, merchants with persistent 4870 chargebacks face:

- **Elevated chargeback ratios** — increasing scrutiny from acquirers
- **Mastercard monitoring programs** — merchants with excessive counterfeit fraud may be placed in the Excessive Chargeback Program or Fraud Program
- **Higher processing rates** — acquirers may reprice accounts with elevated fraud rates

Modern EMV-compliant terminals also support contactless payments (tap-to-pay), which carry the same liability protections as chip and offer faster checkout for customers.

---

## Steps to Achieve EMV Compliance

1. **Audit your terminal fleet** — identify every point-of-sale device and confirm whether it has an integrated chip reader (slot for card insertion, not just a swipe slot)
2. **Confirm chip reading is enabled** — some terminals have chip hardware but chip reading is disabled in software; contact your acquirer or terminal vendor to verify
3. **Update terminal software** — EMV configuration requires certified software; ensure your terminals are running current approved firmware
4. **Train staff** — employees should prompt customers to insert chip cards rather than swipe; many terminals have a configurable "force chip" setting
5. **Test chip transactions** — run a test chip transaction and confirm your authorization record shows POS Entry Mode 05

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: My terminal has a chip slot but the customer swiped — am I still liable?**
A: It depends on your terminal configuration. If the terminal was configured to accept swipe even when a chip is present, you may still face liability. Some terminals are configurable to "force chip fallback" — meaning they will not accept a swipe on a chip card. If your terminal allowed the swipe, the authorization record will show stripe data, and 4870 liability applies.

**Q: What is "fallback" in EMV terminology?**
A: Fallback occurs when a chip card's chip fails to read (due to damage, dirt, or malfunction) and the terminal falls back to reading the magnetic stripe. Fallback transactions carry elevated fraud risk and are sometimes subject to additional issuer scrutiny. Excessive fallback rates can trigger alerts from Mastercard. Merchants should monitor fallback rates and replace or clean terminals that produce frequent chip read failures.

**Q: The customer used a chip card at my counter but my staff swiped it manually. Can I avoid the chargeback?**
A: No. Regardless of why the stripe was used, if a chip-capable card was swiped rather than dipped, the liability shift applies. Train staff to insert chip cards into the chip reader, not swipe them. Most modern terminals can be configured to decline a swipe attempt on chip cards.

**Q: Does 4870 apply to tap-to-pay transactions?**
A: No. Contactless chip transactions (NFC/tap) carry the same EMV liability protection as contact chip transactions, provided the terminal is certified for contactless EMV processing. Contactless magnetic stripe (older tap technology) does not provide the same protection — but this technology is largely obsolete in the current payment ecosystem.

**Q: I just received multiple 4870 chargebacks for the same time period. What happened?**
A: This is a pattern consistent with a skimming incident at your location — a skimming device was likely placed on your terminal, capturing stripe data from multiple cards over a period of time. Contact your acquirer immediately, file a report with your local law enforcement, and inspect your terminal for tampering. Also file a report with Mastercard through your acquirer. Skimming incidents can trigger comprehensive fraud investigations.
