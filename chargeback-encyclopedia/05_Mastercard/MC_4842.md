# Mastercard 4842 — Late Presentment

## Definition

Mastercard reason code 4842 is filed when a merchant submits a transaction for settlement after the permitted timeframe has elapsed. Every card network sets maximum windows within which transactions must be presented for settlement after authorization. Submitting outside these windows results in a late presentment chargeback — regardless of whether the underlying transaction was valid, authorized, and agreed to by the cardholder.

Late presentment is entirely preventable. It is a process failure, not a fraud or dispute scenario. The cardholder may have willingly made the purchase; the chargeback arises solely because the merchant failed to settle in time.

## Mastercard Late Presentment Timelines

Mastercard's presentment deadlines differ by transaction type:

| Transaction Type | Maximum Presentment Timeframe |
|---|---|
| Standard retail (card-present and CNP) | 30 calendar days from transaction date |
| Hospitality (hotel/lodging) | 30 calendar days from checkout date |
| Car rental | 30 calendar days from return/checkout date |
| Cruise line | 30 calendar days from disembarkation |
| Transit (transportation) | 30 calendar days from transaction date |

**Important**: These are Mastercard's maximum windows. Some acquirers impose shorter internal deadlines. Merchants should confirm their acquirer's settlement submission requirements, which may be stricter than Mastercard's rules.

## How Mastercard Timelines Differ from Other Networks

Mastercard's 30-day standard presentment window is relatively consistent across transaction types. Compare this to:

- **Visa**: Generally 7 days for standard card-present transactions, up to 30 days for certain card-not-present and travel-related transactions. Visa's shorter windows catch merchants who delay settlement on in-person transactions.
- **Amex**: Typically 7-20 days depending on merchant category, with stricter enforcement.
- **Discover**: Similar to Visa in structure.

Mastercard's 30-day window is more generous than Visa for standard retail, but the hospitality and rental industry rules create their own complexity. A hotel, for example, must settle within 30 days of checkout — not 30 days from check-in.

## Common Scenarios

**Scenario 1: Delayed Batch Submission**
A small retail merchant runs weekly batch settlements instead of daily. An authorization obtained on the first of the month is included in the batch submitted on the 31st — one day past the 30-day window. A 4842 chargeback is filed for that transaction.

**Scenario 2: Hotel Late Checkout Processing**
A guest checks out of a hotel on the 1st. The front desk is busy and the final folio is not settled until the 35th day. The transaction is outside the 30-day presentment window and a 4842 chargeback is filed.

**Scenario 3: Dispute Delay**
A merchant receives a pre-authorization for a large order. The order is delayed due to supply chain issues, and by the time the goods are shipped and the merchant settles the transaction, 45 days have passed since the original authorization. The presentment is rejected as late.

**Scenario 4: System Failure**
A merchant's payment processor experiences an outage during the settlement batch run. Transactions from that batch are not submitted until the following week. For any transactions that were already approaching the 30-day window, this delay pushes them past the deadline.

**Scenario 5: Authorization Expiry Without Re-Authorization**
A merchant obtained an authorization 25 days ago. Rather than re-authorizing before settling (the authorization is close to expiring), they attempt to settle, and the transaction is flagged as a late presentment.

## Merchant Liability

Merchant liability for 4842 is **absolute in most cases**. There is no fraud defense, no cardholder-related defense, and no argument about who was at fault. If the submission occurred after the permitted window, the chargeback is valid. The limited exceptions:

- **Processor/Acquirer Fault**: If the late submission was caused by a processor or acquirer system failure, the acquirer may absorb the loss. This requires documented evidence of the system failure and confirmation from the processor.
- **Dispute About Transaction Date**: If the merchant can demonstrate that the transaction date the issuer is using to calculate the window is incorrect (e.g., for ongoing services, the service completion date controls, not the charge date), this may form a basis for rebuttal.

## Hospitality Industry Considerations

Hotels, resorts, and vacation rentals have unique challenges with late presentment because:

1. **Extended stays**: A guest checks in on the 1st and checks out on the 10th. The 30-day window starts from checkout (the 10th), not check-in. Settling based on check-in date can create errors.

2. **Dispute resolution delays**: If a guest disputes charges before checkout, resolving the dispute and settling the adjusted amount may take additional time.

3. **No-show charges**: When a guest does not arrive, the no-show charge may be processed days or weeks after the original reservation date. Ensure no-show charges are settled within 30 days of the reservation date.

4. **Batch timing**: If the property management system runs nightly batches but a stay spans month-end, ensure the batch logic correctly handles the checkout date for the settlement window calculation.

## Required Evidence

Given that 4842 liability is largely absolute, the cases where evidence is useful are limited:

1. **Proof of timely submission** (if disputed): Settlement batch file metadata showing submission date, if you believe the date calculation is incorrect.
2. **Corrected transaction date**: Documentation showing the actual transaction or service completion date differs from what the issuer used.
3. **Processor outage documentation**: Written confirmation from your processor that a system failure caused the delay and that the delay was not within the merchant's control.
4. **Service timeline records** (for ongoing service merchants): Evidence showing the service was not completed until a date within the presentment window.

## Winning Strategy

1. **Act immediately upon receiving the chargeback.** If you believe the submission was timely, pull your settlement records and compare the submission date against the transaction date. Calculate the window carefully.

2. **For processor-caused delays**: Contact your processor immediately, get written confirmation of the outage or system failure, and submit this with your Second Presentment. Your acquirer may absorb the loss.

3. **For ongoing service disputes**: If you provide ongoing services (subscriptions, consulting) and the charge date reflects service completion rather than service start, document the service delivery timeline and argue that the presentment window began from the correct date.

4. **Proactive prevention is the winning strategy.** For 4842, preventing the chargeback is far more effective than fighting it. See the prevention checklist below.

## Losing Mistakes

- **Fighting a genuinely late submission without system error evidence**: If you simply failed to settle on time, the chargeback is valid. Accept it and implement process improvements.
- **Confusing authorization expiry with presentment deadline**: These are related but different. An authorization may expire in 30 days, and a late presentment may arise from settling after 30 days. If your authorization has expired, re-authorize before settling.
- **Incorrect date calculations**: Some merchants count business days instead of calendar days, or count from the wrong starting event. Mastercard uses calendar days from the transaction date (or checkout date for hospitality).
- **Ignoring processor SLAs**: If your processor requires daily batch submissions and you run weekly batches, you are operating outside their terms. A late presentment resulting from weekly batch processing is the merchant's fault.

## Prevention Checklist

- Configure your payment system to run settlement batches daily — ideally at the close of business each day.
- Set up automated alerts when transactions approach the 25-day mark without being settled.
- For hospitality: configure your property management system to trigger settlement upon checkout, not upon check-in.
- For high-value pre-authorizations: implement a re-authorization workflow for transactions approaching 25-30 days without settlement.
- Audit authorization-to-settlement timing monthly and flag any transactions settling after 20 days as exceptions.
- For car rental merchants: ensure terminal return workflows automatically trigger same-day settlement.
- Confirm your acquirer's internal batch submission deadlines and configure your systems accordingly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can I re-authorize and re-settle to fix a late presentment before the chargeback is filed?**
A: If you discover the potential late presentment before the chargeback is filed, re-authorizing and settling immediately may prevent the chargeback if the new settlement is timely. However, this carries risk — the cardholder may dispute the re-authorization as an unauthorized charge. Consult your acquirer before re-processing.

**Q: My system shows the batch was submitted on day 29. The issuer says day 31. Who is right?**
A: Request the detailed batch submission record from your processor — this is the authoritative record of when the file was submitted. If your records show timely submission, present this documentation in your Second Presentment and argue the calculation is in error.

**Q: Is 4842 preventable 100% of the time?**
A: Very nearly. The only scenarios outside merchant control are processor or acquirer system failures. All other late presentment scenarios are preventable through proper batch settlement procedures and monitoring. This is one of the most controllable chargeback types.

**Q: If I settle late and the transaction was valid, does the cardholder get their money back even though they made the purchase?**
A: Yes, in most cases. The late presentment chargeback recovers the transaction amount for the cardholder regardless of whether the underlying purchase was legitimate. The merchant absorbs the loss for the process failure.
