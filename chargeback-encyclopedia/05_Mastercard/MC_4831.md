# Mastercard 4831 — Transaction Amount Differs

## Definition

Mastercard reason code 4831 is filed when the amount the cardholder sees debited from their account differs from the amount they agreed to pay at the time of the transaction. More precisely, the settled transaction amount does not match the authorized amount, and there is no valid basis for the difference under Mastercard's rules.

This code covers both accidental errors (pricing mistakes, POS misconfiguration) and intentional but improperly executed adjustments (tip additions, service charge adjustments). It is among the more preventable chargebacks because it almost always stems from a controllable merchant-side process.

## When This Reason Code Applies

4831 applies when:

- The settled amount exceeds the authorized amount beyond Mastercard's permitted tolerance
- A tip was added after authorization without following proper tip adjustment procedures
- A pricing error resulted in the cardholder being charged the wrong amount
- Currency conversion errors altered the final settlement amount
- A service charge, surcharge, or fee was added post-authorization without cardholder consent
- A partial shipment or split fulfillment resulted in the wrong amount being settled

## Authorized Amount vs. Settled Amount — The Core Issue

When a merchant obtains authorization, the issuer approves a specific amount (or an amount within a defined tolerance). Mastercard permits merchants to settle for an amount that differs from the authorization within these tolerances:

| Merchant Category | Permitted Variance |
|---|---|
| Restaurant/Bar (tip adjustment) | 20% above authorized amount |
| Hotel/Lodging | 15% above authorized amount (for taxes, incidentals) |
| Car Rental | 15% above authorized amount |
| All other retail | Must match exactly (within minor rounding) |

If the settled amount exceeds these tolerances without a new authorization, the issuer has grounds to file a 4831 chargeback for the difference — or in some cases the full transaction amount.

## Common Scenarios

**Scenario 1: Unauthorized Tip Addition**
A restaurant authorizes $45.00 for a dinner. The cardholder signs a paper receipt and writes in a $10.00 tip, making the total $55.00. The server adds the tip, and the total settles at $55.00, which is within the 20% tolerance ($45 × 1.20 = $54.00). However, the cardholder disputes the amount claiming they wrote $5, not $10. This generates a 4831 dispute.

**Scenario 2: Pricing Error at POS**
An e-commerce merchant lists a product at $89.99 but due to a website error, the customer is charged $899.99. The customer sees the correct price in the cart but is billed an order of magnitude more. This is a clear 4831 scenario.

**Scenario 3: Post-Authorization Service Charge**
A hotel authorizes $200 for a one-night stay. At checkout, the hotel adds a $50 resort fee that was not disclosed during booking. The total settles at $250. The cardholder disputes the $50 difference as an unauthorized amount.

**Scenario 4: Tip Adjustment Above Tolerance**
A bar authorizes $30 for drinks. A large tip of $25 is added, settling the total at $55 — an 83% increase over the authorization. This exceeds the 20% restaurant tolerance and creates a 4831 liability for the difference.

**Scenario 5: Split Shipment Error**
An online merchant splits an order into two shipments and settles twice — once for the correct amount and once with an error in the second batch, resulting in a total amount that differs from what the customer authorized.

## Merchant Liability

Merchant liability is **high** when:
- The settled amount genuinely differs from what the cardholder agreed to pay
- Tip adjustment exceeds the permitted tolerance percentage
- An undisclosed fee was added post-authorization
- A pricing error occurred on the merchant's systems

Merchant liability may be **reduced or eliminated** when:
- The signed receipt clearly shows the amount including the tip, and the settlement matches the signed receipt
- The cardholder consented to additional charges (documented in writing)
- The variance is within Mastercard's permitted tolerance for the merchant category

## Required Evidence

To contest a 4831 chargeback, provide:

1. **Signed Sales Receipt or Order Confirmation**: Shows the amount the cardholder agreed to, including any tip or additional charges.
2. **Authorization Record**: The authorization approval and the amount for which approval was obtained.
3. **Settlement Record**: The exact amount settled and how it was calculated.
4. **Tip Line Documentation**: For restaurant transactions, the original signed receipt with the tip amount the customer wrote — legibly photographed or scanned.
5. **Disclosure Records**: For hotels or rental companies adding incidentals or resort fees, evidence that these charges were disclosed to the cardholder at the time of booking or check-in (e.g., booking confirmation with fee schedule, signed check-in agreement).
6. **Pricing Evidence**: If disputing a pricing error claim, screenshots of the actual price shown at time of purchase, cart confirmation, and order confirmation email.

## Winning Strategy

1. **Present the signed receipt with matching amounts.** A clearly legible signed receipt where the cardholder wrote in the tip and total amount, matching the settled amount, is your strongest defense. The cardholder's signature is acknowledgment of the amount.

2. **Calculate and document tolerance compliance.** Show mathematically that the settled amount is within Mastercard's permitted variance for your merchant category. Include the authorization amount, the tolerance calculation, and the settled amount.

3. **For fee disputes, show pre-authorization disclosure.** If you added a resort fee, damage waiver, or service charge, prove it was disclosed before the transaction was completed — preferably in a signed agreement or booking confirmation.

4. **Photograph tip receipts.** Restaurant and bar merchants should photograph every signed receipt at close of shift and retain images for 18 months. This eliminates tip fraud disputes cold.

## Losing Mistakes

- **Settling for amounts that genuinely exceed tolerance**: If a tip was added incorrectly or the wrong amount was charged, the chargeback is valid. Refund the difference proactively before the chargeback escalates.
- **Unable to produce the signed receipt**: Without the signed receipt showing the cardholder-written amount, you have no defense against a tip dispute.
- **Adding hidden fees post-authorization**: Undisclosed fees are both a chargeback risk and a potential regulatory issue. All fees must be disclosed before authorization.
- **Assuming the authorization amount controls**: In a dispute, the amount the cardholder agreed to (documented on the receipt) governs — not just what was authorized. Both must align.

## Prevention Checklist

- Configure POS systems to capture and store tip amounts separately from base amounts.
- For table-service restaurants, implement electronic tip capture (digital receipts) to eliminate handwritten tip disputes.
- Print authorization amount on customer-facing receipts so cardholders can verify at point of signature.
- Disclose all fees (resort fees, service charges, damage waivers) in writing before the transaction is processed.
- For e-commerce, implement cart and checkout amount validation to catch pricing errors before settlement.
- Audit settlement batches daily against authorization amounts to catch variance errors before cardholders notice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: The customer clearly wrote $15 as the tip, but is now claiming they wrote $5. How do I win?**
A: Provide a clear photographic copy of the original signed receipt showing the tip amount and total. If your handwriting interpretation is clear and consistent with the total the customer also wrote, this is your winning evidence. Some acquirers will have handwriting analysis support for high-value disputes.

**Q: My e-commerce pricing had a bug that charged the wrong amount. The customer got a correct order confirmation but was charged more. What do I do?**
A: Issue a refund immediately for the overcharge. Then evaluate whether the 4831 chargeback was already filed. If you issue the refund before the chargeback is filed, you may prevent it. If the chargeback has already been filed, submit evidence of the refund along with your rebuttal.

**Q: Can I add a fuel surcharge or convenience fee without re-authorizing?**
A: Only if the surcharge was disclosed to the cardholder before the original authorization and the total amount (including the surcharge) was included in the original authorization request. Undisclosed post-authorization additions create 4831 liability.

**Q: What is the tolerance for standard retail transactions?**
A: Standard retail (non-restaurant, non-lodging, non-rental) transactions must settle for the exact authorized amount. Any variance, even small, can trigger a 4831. If the final amount is unknown at authorization time (e.g., variable weight items), use incremental authorization or an estimated amount and re-authorize.
