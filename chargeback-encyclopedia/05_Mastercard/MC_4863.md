# Mastercard 4863 — Cardholder Does Not Recognize

## What This Reason Code Means

Mastercard reason code 4863 is triggered when a cardholder reviews their statement and sees a charge they do not recognize. Unlike reason code 4837 (No Cardholder Authorization), which is an explicit fraud allegation, 4863 occupies a middle ground: the cardholder is not necessarily claiming fraud — they simply cannot place the transaction. They may have genuinely forgotten a purchase, may not recognize your business name on their statement, or may be confused about the date or amount.

This distinction matters strategically. A 4863 dispute is often more winnable than an outright fraud claim because the bar for the merchant's evidence is proportionally lower — you do not need to prove the cardholder authorized the transaction under duress; you need to show that the transaction is recognizable and consistent with their purchase history.

The most common cause of 4863 disputes is **a confusing or truncated billing descriptor**. In the majority of cases, the cardholder would recognize the transaction if the descriptor clearly identified your business. This makes 4863 heavily preventable.

## The Billing Descriptor Problem

Your billing descriptor is the name that appears on the cardholder's bank statement next to the charge. If it reads "SQSP*12345" instead of "ACME DESIGN STUDIO," the cardholder has no idea who charged them.

Common descriptor problems:
- **Parent company name, not brand name** — the cardholder knows "Shopify Store," not "6958447 ONTARIO INC"
- **Truncated names** — card networks limit descriptors to 22–25 characters; longer names get cut off awkwardly
- **Generic payment platform names** — some platforms display their own name rather than the merchant's
- **No geographic indicator** — international merchants without a recognizable country suffix confuse cardholders
- **Outdated descriptor** — the business name changed but the payment processor was never updated

### How to Audit and Fix Your Billing Descriptor

Log into your payment processor's settings and verify your descriptor exactly as it appears to cardholders. A well-structured descriptor typically combines:

- **Business name** (recognizable, abbreviated if necessary)
- **Website URL or short product name** (helps cardholders self-identify)
- **Phone number** (allows cardholders to call instead of dispute)

Example of a strong descriptor: `ACME DESIGN * 555-000-1234`

The phone number is particularly valuable — cardholders who can call and confirm the charge almost never file a dispute. This one change alone can reduce 4863 chargebacks by 30–50%.

## 4863 vs. 4837: Understanding the Distinction

| Dimension | 4863 — Does Not Recognize | 4837 — No Cardholder Authorization |
|---|---|---|
| Cardholder claim | "I don't recognize this charge" | "I never made this transaction — fraud" |
| Implied intent | Confusion or memory lapse | Explicit fraud allegation |
| Evidence threshold | Lower — prior history and clear data sufficient | Higher — must demonstrate authorization |
| Win probability | Higher for merchants | Lower for merchants |
| Common cause | Descriptor confusion, family member purchase | Account compromise, CNP fraud |

When you receive a 4863, do not treat it as a confirmed fraud dispute. It is a request for identification. Your response should reintroduce the transaction clearly — show who made it, when, and why it matches the cardholder's account.

## Evidence That Wins 4863 Disputes

The goal is to give the issuing bank enough information to present to the cardholder and allow them to say "oh, yes, I remember that."

### Transaction Data

- **IP address** of the order — matches cardholder's known location, ISP, or prior order IP
- **Device fingerprint** — device ID or browser signature linked to prior purchases from the same account
- **Account login records** — showing the order was placed through the cardholder's registered account
- **Shipping address** — delivery to the billing address or a previously used address on the account

### Prior Purchase History

This is often your strongest evidence. If the cardholder has placed three previous orders in the last 12 months from the same card, with similar amounts, that pattern is highly consistent with a legitimate purchase. Submit a summary of prior order dates, amounts, and products.

### AVS and CVV Results

- **AVS match** — the billing address provided at checkout matched the card's registered address
- **CVV2 match** — the 3-digit security code was correctly entered at checkout

These do not prove the cardholder placed the order themselves, but they indicate the transaction was not a simple account number compromise — the physical card (or its data) was present or accurately reproduced.

### Communication Records

If the cardholder contacted your support team, placed a follow-up order, or engaged with a delivery confirmation for this transaction, those records are compelling. A cardholder who emailed asking about their order status cannot credibly claim to not recognize the transaction.

### Order Confirmation and Account Email

Show that an order confirmation was sent to the email address on the cardholder's account, and that the email was not bounced or marked undeliverable. If the cardholder received and opened the email, tracking pixel data can confirm engagement.

## Building Your Rebuttal Letter

A rebuttal for 4863 should do three things:

1. **Identify the transaction clearly** — date, amount, order number, product purchased
2. **Connect the transaction to the cardholder** — their account, their device, their shipping address, their prior purchase pattern
3. **Explain the billing descriptor** — if there is any chance of descriptor confusion, explicitly note what your descriptor says and why it corresponds to the transaction in question

Keep the letter to one page. Attach evidence as labeled exhibits ("Exhibit A: Prior Purchase History," "Exhibit B: AVS Match Confirmation").

## Improving Your Descriptor to Prevent 4863 Recurrence

If 4863 chargebacks represent a recurring pattern in your dispute data, a descriptor audit is mandatory. Steps:

1. **Contact your acquirer or payment processor** to update your soft descriptor (the descriptor that appears on card statements for online transactions)
2. **Test the descriptor** by running a $1 test charge on a personal card and checking your statement
3. **Add a customer service phone number** to your descriptor wherever possible
4. **Include your URL** as the second part of a dual-field descriptor (available with most modern processors)
5. **Match your descriptor to your brand name**, not your legal entity name

For platforms with multi-seller marketplaces, work with your platform provider to ensure sub-merchant descriptors are correctly configured.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: A family member used the cardholder's card without telling them. Is this a 4863 I can win?**
A: Yes, this is a common scenario. If the shipping address is the same household, the device matches a prior order, and prior purchases are documented, most issuers will accept that the transaction was made by an authorized account holder or household member. The cardholder should recognize the purchase when presented with this evidence.

**Q: My descriptor is clear, but I'm still getting 4863s. What else could cause this?**
A: Other common causes include: transactions that occurred weeks before the statement arrived (the gap causes memory lapses), small recurring charges that don't match the expected billing cycle, or purchases made while traveling that appear in an unexpected currency or merchant category code. Review your dispute data for patterns — time-gap cases may benefit from including more context in your order confirmation emails.

**Q: How is a 4863 different from friendly fraud?**
A: Friendly fraud is when a cardholder deliberately disputes a transaction they know they made. 4863 can be genuine confusion or deliberate misuse. You respond the same way regardless — with transaction evidence. The issuer's decision often comes down to the quality of your data. If the cardholder genuinely forgot the purchase, good evidence resolves it. If it is deliberate, strong evidence still improves your win odds, though not to 100%.

**Q: Can I fight a 4863 if the transaction was placed by a secondary cardholder on the same account?**
A: Yes. A secondary or authorized cardholder's purchase is the primary cardholder's responsibility. If the transaction was placed using a card in the account, document this and note that authorized users on the account have card-making authority. Include any evidence linking the secondary cardholder to the transaction.

**Q: What AVS result should I require to minimize 4863 exposure?**
A: At minimum, require a full AVS match (billing address line and zip code both match) and CVV match for all CNP transactions. Do not accept orders where both AVS and CVV fail simultaneously — this significantly increases your dispute exposure across all fraud and unrecognized codes.
