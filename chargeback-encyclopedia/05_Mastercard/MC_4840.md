# Mastercard 4840 — Fraudulent Processing of Transactions

## Definition

Mastercard reason code 4840 is one of the most serious codes a merchant can receive. Unlike most chargeback codes that arise from customer disputes or technical errors, 4840 implies that the merchant itself may be engaged in or complicit in fraudulent activity. It is issued when multiple fraudulent transactions have been processed in a pattern suggesting the merchant is a source of compromise, a participant in transaction laundering, or otherwise facilitating fraud rather than simply being a victim of it.

This code can be the precursor to MATCH (Member Alert to Control High-Risk Merchants) listing, account termination, and investigation by Mastercard's security division.

## When This Reason Code Applies

4840 applies when:

- **Transaction Laundering (Factoring)**: A merchant processes transactions on behalf of an undisclosed third-party business — a violation of Mastercard's rules that allows non-registered businesses to accept card payments without proper due diligence.
- **Card Skimming at Merchant Location**: Card data is stolen at the merchant's point of sale (via a skimming device installed on the terminal) and used fraudulently. The merchant location is the common point of compromise.
- **Employee Fraud**: An employee is processing unauthorized transactions on customers' cards without their knowledge.
- **Systematic Multiple Fraudulent Transactions**: A pattern of fraudulent transactions across multiple cardholders' accounts all connected to the same merchant, suggesting the merchant's systems or operations are the source of the fraud.
- **Counterfeit Card Use Across Multiple Accounts**: Multiple counterfeit cards all linked to a data breach at the merchant's location are used and then disputed.

## Transaction Laundering — The High-Risk Scenario

Transaction laundering (also called factoring or third-party processing) is the practice of processing payment card transactions for a business that has not been approved for card acceptance by an acquiring bank. Common examples:

- A brick-and-mortar retail merchant secretly processes transactions for an online gambling, pharmaceutical, or firearms business through their approved merchant account
- A small business owner accepts payments on behalf of friends or family who don't have merchant accounts
- A merchant submits fictitious transactions to move money through the card network

Transaction laundering is a significant fraud risk because the acquirer and the card networks cannot assess the risk profile of the undisclosed business. When discovered — either through fraud patterns, chargeback analysis, or cardholder complaints — the result is typically 4840 chargebacks, MATCH listing, and potential investigation by law enforcement.

## Card Skimming — Common Point of Compromise

When Mastercard's fraud analysis systems identify that multiple compromised cards share a common point of use — a specific merchant location — that merchant may receive 4840 chargebacks even if the merchant is a victim of skimming rather than a perpetrator. Indicators of skimming-related 4840:

- Sudden spike in chargebacks from multiple cardholders who all transacted at the same location
- Fraudulent charges appear on accounts in geographically distant locations shortly after the legitimate transaction at the merchant
- Physical evidence of a skimming device found on the merchant's terminal

In this scenario, the merchant is typically a victim, but they are still the common point of compromise and face chargeback liability. The key action is to immediately report the potential skimming to the acquiring bank, law enforcement, and Mastercard's security team.

## MATCH List Risk

The MATCH (Member Alert to Control High-Risk Merchants) list is Mastercard's database of terminated merchants and their principals. Reasons for MATCH listing include:

- Reason code 4840 chargebacks indicating merchant fraud
- Excessive chargebacks beyond monitoring program thresholds
- Transaction laundering
- Processing violations
- Bankruptcy or fraud-related terminations

MATCH listing is shared across the payments industry. A merchant listed on MATCH will find it extremely difficult — often impossible — to obtain a new merchant account with any acquiring bank. **The principals of the business (owners, officers) are also listed by name**, making this a career-defining consequence.

## Mastercard Security Monitoring

Mastercard's Fraud Intelligence and Security Operations team actively monitors merchant transaction patterns. Indicators that may trigger a 4840 investigation:

- Unusually high transaction volumes for the merchant's category and size
- Transactions with atypical amounts, frequencies, or geographic distributions
- Sudden changes in average transaction size or customer base
- High rates of manual key-entry transactions (common in laundering scenarios)
- Transactions at unusual hours inconsistent with the merchant's business type

## Merchant Liability and Defense Options

Merchant liability in 4840 scenarios is very high, and defense options are limited depending on the specific allegation:

### If the Merchant is a Victim of Skimming
**Defense is possible but requires:**
- Immediate reporting to acquiring bank and law enforcement
- Evidence that a skimming device was installed without merchant knowledge (often requires physical inspection)
- Documentation of your POS security protocols
- Cooperation with the acquiring bank's investigation

### If the Merchant Processed Legitimate Transactions
**Defense requires:**
- Demonstrating that each disputed transaction was a legitimate, authorized purchase
- Providing transaction evidence for each dispute individually
- Showing that the pattern of disputes is coincidental rather than indicative of a systematic problem

### If Transaction Laundering is Alleged
**Defense options are extremely limited.** Transaction laundering is a violation of your merchant agreement. Cooperation with the acquirer's investigation is the only viable path, and termination of the merchant account is likely.

## Required Evidence

For each disputed transaction under 4840:

1. **Authorization records** with approval codes for each transaction
2. **Business records** proving the transaction was a legitimate sale from your registered business
3. **Customer records** — receipts, invoices, delivery confirmation
4. **POS security documentation** — evidence of tamper-evident seals, regular terminal inspections, employee background checks
5. **Incident report** if skimming device was discovered
6. **Law enforcement report number** if criminal complaint was filed
7. **Employee records** if investigating internal fraud

## Winning Strategy

1. **Immediately engage your acquiring bank.** 4840 requires coordination beyond a standard chargeback response. Contact your acquirer's risk management team, not just the dispute processing department.

2. **For skimming victims: document and report.** File a police report, obtain the incident report number, document the skimming device discovery (photos, forensics report), and provide this to your acquirer. This demonstrates you are a victim, not a perpetrator.

3. **For each individual transaction**: Build a standard fraud chargeback response as you would for 4837 — authorization records, delivery proof, customer evidence. You must address each disputed transaction individually.

4. **Engage legal counsel.** If 4840 chargebacks are accompanied by an investigation or account suspension notice from your acquirer, consult a payments attorney before responding.

5. **Conduct an internal security audit.** Proactively assess your POS terminals, employee access controls, and transaction monitoring systems. Show your acquirer that you are taking corrective action.

## Losing Mistakes

- **Ignoring the chargebacks or the acquiring bank's outreach**: 4840 investigations can move quickly. Non-response is interpreted as admission.
- **Attempting to re-route processing to a new acquirer without disclosure**: If your account is under investigation, switching to a new acquirer without disclosure violates Mastercard rules.
- **Continuing to operate in the same manner**: If skimming or employee fraud is suspected, continuing normal operations while the investigation proceeds can increase liability.
- **Fighting individual chargebacks without addressing the systemic issue**: You can win individual disputes on evidence, but if the systemic pattern is not addressed, MATCH listing is still possible.

## Prevention Checklist

- Inspect POS terminals and PIN pads daily for signs of tampering or skimming devices.
- Use tamper-evident seals on terminals and document inspection dates.
- Implement P2PE (Point-to-Point Encryption) to render skimmed card data useless.
- Conduct employee background checks before granting access to payment systems.
- Never process transactions for third parties — every transaction must be for your own registered business.
- Monitor transaction patterns and set alerts for unusual volumes or patterns.
- Train staff to recognize social engineering attempts that might lead to laundering scenarios.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: I received 4840 chargebacks but I'm a legitimate business. Can I fight these?**
A: Yes, and you should. Each transaction disputed under 4840 should be addressed with the same evidence you would use for a 4837 (fraud) dispute: authorization records, delivery proof, and customer evidence. The key difference is that the acquirer may also launch a separate investigation into your business operations.

**Q: My employee stole card data and processed unauthorized transactions. Am I liable?**
A: Likely yes, unless you can demonstrate that you had robust security controls in place that the employee circumvented through extraordinary means. File a police report, terminate the employee, and work with your acquirer on a remediation plan. Employee theft is not a defense against cardholder chargebacks.

**Q: What is the MATCH list and how do I get off it?**
A: The MATCH list is a shared industry database. Once listed, removal typically requires the original acquirer that listed you to submit a removal request, and only in specific circumstances (e.g., the listing was made in error). Listings generally remain for 5 years. Prevention is far preferable to remediation.

**Q: How do I prove my business is legitimate and I'm not laundering transactions?**
A: Maintain complete business records — business registration, tax filings, inventory records, customer records, and a clear alignment between your merchant category code, your actual business activities, and your transaction patterns. Acquirers and card networks scrutinize the alignment between stated business type and actual transaction data.
