## Overview

Amex reason code C31 — "Goods or Services Not as Described or Defective" — is filed when a cardholder receives goods or services that do not match what was advertised, ordered, or represented at the time of sale. Unlike a non-delivery dispute, the cardholder in a C31 case received something — they are disputing the nature or quality of what they received, not its absence. C31 covers the full spectrum from outright misrepresentation (product is a completely different item from what was listed) to quality disputes (product arrived damaged or stopped functioning within a short period).

This code applies to physical goods, digital goods, and services alike. A product with photos that significantly misrepresent its actual appearance, a service delivered at a materially lower standard than was advertised, a digital product missing features from the description, or a custom order that does not conform to agreed specifications — all fall under C31. The breadth of the code makes it one of the more nuanced dispute categories: the line between a legitimate mismatch claim and a buyer's remorse claim dressed as a dispute is often contested, and the quality of the merchant's original product description documentation determines which side of that line Amex draws.

Amex holds merchants to a higher standard on C31 than Visa does under its equivalent code 13.3. Amex is more receptive to cardholder claims of misrepresentation and expects merchants to document their product descriptions thoroughly and retain that documentation as a standard business practice. Merchants who rely on aspirational product photography, minimal specifications, or verbal representations without written confirmation are structurally vulnerable to C31 disputes.

## Common Scenarios

**Product materially different from listing photos.** A cardholder orders a piece of furniture based on website photos showing a solid wood frame and neutral upholstery. The delivered item has a particleboard frame and noticeably different fabric color. The cardholder contacts the merchant, receives no satisfactory resolution, and files a C31. Without pre-shipment photos or a detailed specification listing matching what was shipped, this is a difficult dispute for the merchant.

**Arrived damaged in transit.** A cardholder receives electronics or fragile goods that are broken, cracked, or non-functional on arrival. The damage occurred during shipping, but the cardholder disputes under C31 rather than contacting the carrier. The merchant must prove the item left their facility undamaged to shift liability to the carrier.

**Service materially below advertised standard.** A cardholder paid for premium catering services advertised with professional-grade food photography and specific menu commitments. The actual service delivered significantly lower-quality food and omitted several advertised menu items. The cardholder disputes the charge as the service not matching the advertised description.

**Custom order does not match specifications.** A cardholder paid for bespoke custom goods — furniture, apparel, signage — with agreed specifications. The delivered items do not conform to the specifications on dimensions, materials, or finishes. If the merchant followed a written specification document signed by both parties, this is defensible; if specifications were communicated verbally or via informal messages, it is more contested.

**Digital product missing advertised features.** A cardholder purchased software or an online course that was advertised with specific features or content modules. The product as delivered is missing those features or contains significantly different content. The cardholder disputes under C31.

## Merchant Liability

**Merchant is liable when:**
- The product delivered materially differs from the written description, photographs, or specification document provided at the time of sale.
- The item arrived damaged and the merchant cannot demonstrate it was packed and shipped in good condition.
- The service was delivered at a materially lower standard than what was advertised or contractually agreed.
- The product description was vague, relied on aspirational photography, or omitted material information (material composition, country of origin, actual dimensions) that a reasonable buyer would rely on.
- The cardholder attempted to return the item within the stated return window and the merchant failed to process the return or refund appropriately.

**Merchant is NOT liable when:**
- The product description was accurate, detailed, and complete, and the delivered item matches the stated specifications within normal tolerances.
- The cardholder's dissatisfaction is based on a change of mind, personal preference, or failure to read the product specifications — not a genuine mismatch.
- The cardholder never contacted the merchant before filing the dispute and never attempted to use the return or exchange process.
- The goods were sold as "used," "open box," or "as-is" with condition clearly disclosed at the time of purchase, and the received condition matches the disclosed description.
- Three months or more of product use preceded the dispute — extended use strongly undermines a claim that the product was "not as described."

## Required Evidence

- **Original product listing screenshots** with timestamps showing the product title, description copy, all photos, size charts, material specifications, and condition disclosures as they appeared at the time of purchase — including any "Wayback Machine" archive if the listing was subsequently updated.
- **Pre-shipment photos or video** of the item taken before packing and dispatch, showing condition and appearance matching the listing.
- **Quality control records** for the batch or lot from which the item was shipped, confirming conformance to specifications.
- **Shipping record** showing the item left the merchant's facility in good condition — carrier receipt with weight matching the expected item weight and packaging specifications.
- **CRM or support records** showing no complaint, return request, or contact from this cardholder prior to the chargeback — demonstrating the cardholder did not attempt merchant resolution before disputing.
- **Return policy** displayed at checkout, showing the policy was visible before the cardholder completed the purchase — including return window, condition requirements, and refund or exchange options.
- **Custom order specification document** (for bespoke goods) showing the agreed specifications with date and cardholder acknowledgment.
- **SKU and fulfillment confirmation** showing the item shipped matches the product the cardholder ordered — ruling out a fulfillment error where a different SKU was picked.

## Winning Strategy

The merchant's core defense for C31 is an accurate, complete, and contemporaneously documented product description paired with evidence that the item shipped matched that description. The most effective rebuttal leads with the product listing as it existed at the time of purchase — screenshots with timestamps — and follows with pre-shipment photos showing the actual item sent.

If the cardholder never contacted the merchant before filing the dispute, this is significant and should be highlighted prominently in the response. Amex's rules for C31 generally expect cardholders to attempt merchant resolution — including a return or exchange attempt — before disputing. A cardholder who skipped this step and went directly to Amex weakens their own claim. Present your return policy to show that a remedy was available and the cardholder chose not to use it.

For service disputes, shift from product photos to service documentation: the contract or service agreement showing what was agreed, any written communications during the service delivery confirming the scope, photos or records from the service event, and staff logs. If the cardholder signed off on the service or provided positive feedback during or immediately after delivery, include that documentation — it is powerful evidence that the service met expectations at the time of delivery.

## Common Mistakes

**Aspirational product photography without accurate description text.** High-quality studio photography that makes a product appear significantly better than it actually is — through professional lighting, color correction, or selective angles — is a structural C31 liability. The written description must accurately represent what a buyer will receive, and photos should be representative rather than aspirational.

**Vague product specifications.** Listings that say "premium materials" without specifying materials, or "approximately 30cm" without precise dimensions, create gaps that C31 disputes exploit. Complete specifications — materials, dimensions, weight, country of manufacture, compatibility — leave less room for "not as described" claims.

**No cardholder contact before the chargeback.** If the cardholder never emailed, called, or submitted a return request, merchants sometimes fail to mention this in their response. This is a significant factor in Amex's evaluation — note it explicitly.

**Shipping fragile goods without pre-shipment documentation.** For high-value or fragile items, not photographing the item before packing leaves the merchant unable to demonstrate the damage occurred in transit rather than at origin. Pre-shipment photography takes seconds and eliminates a category of unwinnable disputes.

**Return policy not visible at checkout.** If the return policy requires three clicks from the product listing to find, and is not shown during the checkout flow, Amex will not credit you with having provided a remedy. The policy must be visible before payment is completed.

## Timeline

| Milestone | Timeframe |
|---|---|
| Cardholder files dispute with Amex | Within 120 days of transaction or delivery date |
| Amex notifies merchant of chargeback | Within a few business days of dispute filing |
| **Merchant response deadline** | **20 calendar days from chargeback notification** |
| Amex decision after merchant response | Typically 4–8 weeks |
| Pre-arbitration (if escalated) | Additional 30 days per stage |

For C31 disputes involving physical goods, the 20-day response window requires merchants to already have pre-shipment photos, SKU records, and product description archives readily accessible. Merchants in high-SKU retail environments should maintain a systematic evidence archive — including product listing snapshots and shipment photos — that can be retrieved within hours, not days.

## FAQs

**Q: The cardholder says the product looks different from the photos, but we did not edit the photos. How do we prove that?**
A: Submit the original high-resolution source photos with file metadata showing the creation date, camera device, and file size. If you use a product photography service, request the unedited RAW files from the shoot. Submit multiple angles and a ruler or reference object in at least one photo to establish actual scale. If your listing is still live and the photos are accurate, a live screenshot with the URL and date is useful context.

**Q: A customer built a custom product exactly to our written specification but says the result wasn't what they "envisioned." Are we liable?**
A: Generally no, if you built to the agreed written specification. The specification document — containing every agreed detail the cardholder approved — is your primary evidence. If the cardholder also approved a sample, prototype, or digital mockup before full production, include that approval record. C31 requires a mismatch from what was described; if the specification was accurate and fully executed, the dispute fails on its own terms.

**Q: An item arrived damaged in transit. Who is liable — us or the carrier?**
A: The merchant is liable to the cardholder for the dispute. Whether the carrier owes the merchant reimbursement is a separate matter. To shift focus to the carrier, the merchant must demonstrate the item left the facility undamaged — pre-shipment photos, weight record, packaging specifications. If you can demonstrate this, you can accept the C31, refund the cardholder, and pursue a carrier insurance claim separately. If you cannot demonstrate the item left undamaged, the C31 liability rests with you.

**Q: The cardholder used the product for three months and then filed a C31 claiming it was "not as described." Is that within Amex's window?**
A: Amex's filing window allows disputes for some period after the transaction date, but extended use before the dispute is factual evidence that the product met the cardholder's expectations at the time of purchase. Include any usage records — warranty registration, account activity, reorder history — that demonstrate the cardholder used and accepted the product. Three months of use before claiming "not as described" is a very weak factual foundation for the cardholder and should be highlighted prominently in your response.

## Key Takeaways

- C31 is filed when the cardholder received goods or services that materially differed from what was described or were defective — it requires that something was received, unlike non-delivery disputes.
- Accurate, complete, and archived product descriptions are the foundation of every C31 defense — aspirational photography and vague specifications are structural liabilities.
- Amex holds merchants to a higher standard on C31 than Visa does under 13.3 — complete documentation and thorough description accuracy are required, not optional.
- If the cardholder never attempted a return or contacted merchant support before filing, highlight this fact in your response — Amex's rules expect a resolution attempt before escalating to a dispute.
- Pre-shipment photos of items before packing is the single most effective operational practice for managing transit-damage C31 disputes.
