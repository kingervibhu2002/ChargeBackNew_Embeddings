# Visa 13.3 — Not as Described or Defective Merchandise

Visa reason code 13.3 covers quality and accuracy disputes — situations where the cardholder received the goods or services they paid for, but claims the item was defective, damaged, or materially different from how the merchant described it. Unlike 13.1 (not received), the cardholder in a 13.3 dispute is not denying they received something — they are saying what they received was not what they agreed to buy.

## What This Chargeback Means

A 13.3 chargeback can arise from several types of complaints:

**Product did not match the description:** The cardholder ordered a "genuine leather wallet" and received a synthetic material product. They ordered a "32GB USB drive" and received an 8GB drive. The physical product differs materially from the merchant's description in a way that affected the purchase decision.

**Item was defective:** The product arrived broken, malfunctioning, or unusable — not due to shipping damage, but due to a quality defect inherent to the item itself.

**Item was damaged in transit:** The product arrived damaged, and the merchant is held responsible either because the packaging was inadequate or because the merchant assumed responsibility for in-transit damage.

**Wrong item shipped:** The merchant shipped a different product than what was ordered — a different color, model, size, or SKU that the cardholder did not request.

**Services not performed as described:** In a service context, the service was performed but not in the manner or to the standard described in the service agreement.

## Who Bears Liability

The merchant bears liability for 13.3 chargebacks when the product or service genuinely did not match the representation. The cardholder does not need to prove malicious intent — an honest misdescription still creates liability.

However, liability shifts back to the cardholder when:
- The product matched its description and the cardholder simply changed their mind or had different expectations
- The cardholder did not attempt to contact the merchant for resolution before filing the chargeback
- The merchant's return policy was clearly displayed and the cardholder did not follow it

## Evidence You Need to Fight This Chargeback

### Original Product Description as Displayed at Time of Purchase

Submit a screenshot or archived copy of the product listing, website page, or catalog entry as it existed when the cardholder made the purchase. This is the cornerstone of your defense — you are comparing what you described against what you delivered.

**Important:** The description must match what was actually shipped. If your product page showed a red item and you shipped blue, the 13.3 chargeback is valid. If your page showed blue and you shipped blue, the description matched.

**Archive your product pages.** Use the Wayback Machine (web.archive.org), internal page snapshots, or a page archiving service to capture periodic screenshots of your product listings. Without an archived version, you cannot prove what the product page said at the time of purchase — the cardholder could claim the description was different.

### Photos of the Item Shipped

If you maintain packing records or warehouse photos, submit images of the specific item packed and shipped for this order. This is particularly powerful when the cardholder claims they received the wrong item — if you have a photo of the correct item being packed with the order slip visible, the claim is much harder to sustain.

**Implementation:** Some warehouse operations photograph items and packing slips together as standard quality control. This practice has significant chargeback defense value beyond its operational purpose.

### Packing and Shipping Records

The packing slip, warehouse pick record, or shipping manifest showing the specific SKU and item description that was packed for this order. Cross-reference with the cardholder's order to show the correct item was selected and shipped.

### Return Policy and Whether the Customer Attempted Return

Visa 13.3 rules require that the cardholder give the merchant an opportunity to resolve the issue before filing a chargeback — typically by attempting a return or exchange. Submit:
- Your return policy as displayed on your website (screenshot)
- Any return request received from the cardholder
- Whether a return was offered, whether a return label was provided, and whether the item was returned

If the cardholder never contacted you before filing the chargeback, note this explicitly in your rebuttal. Under Visa rules, the cardholder is generally required to attempt to resolve the issue with the merchant first.

### Customer Service Communication Log

All email, chat, and phone interaction records with the cardholder related to the dispute. If the cardholder contacted you claiming the item was wrong, how you responded matters:
- Did you offer a replacement or refund?
- Was your offer declined?
- Did the cardholder make unreasonable demands before filing?

## When the Merchant Wins

Merchants typically win 13.3 chargebacks when:

- **Product matched the description precisely:** Measurement or specification disputes where your listing clearly stated the specifications that match the item shipped
- **Cardholder's expectation was unreasonable:** The description was accurate but the cardholder imagined something different from what was described
- **Cardholder never attempted a return or resolution:** Visa rules require a resolution attempt; skipping this step weakens the cardholder's position
- **Return was offered and refused:** If you offered a full refund or replacement and the cardholder declined and filed a chargeback instead, that rejection of a reasonable offer is relevant context
- **Used item claimed as defective:** If your listings clearly indicate items are "used" or "refurbished" and the cardholder's "defect" complaint reflects normal wear for a used item, the description was accurate

## When the Merchant Loses

Merchants typically lose 13.3 chargebacks when:

- **The item was clearly defective from the factory:** A device that won't power on, a garment with a structural defect, a sealed item missing components
- **The wrong item was shipped due to a picking error:** Documentation shows order was for one SKU but a different SKU was shipped
- **Product significantly underperformed its described specifications:** A "waterproof" item that leaks at normal use conditions when the listing claimed waterproofing
- **Quality was grossly below described standard:** A "premium" product that arrived with obvious poor quality materials when the listing represented high quality

## Quality Dispute Strategy

Quality disputes are inherently subjective. "Not as described" is clearer — either the specifications match or they do not. "Defective" involves more interpretation. To navigate quality disputes:

1. Offer a replacement or refund immediately upon receiving a quality complaint — this prevents the chargeback entirely
2. If fighting the dispute, focus on the factual description versus the item delivered, not on defending quality subjectively
3. Use supplier or manufacturer documentation (product data sheets, quality certifications) to establish the expected specifications of the item you sold

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: A cardholder claims my product is defective, but it worked fine when I tested it. What do I do?**
A: Subjective defect claims are difficult. If the product has measurable specifications (a scale, a battery life measurement, a speed test), providing technical documentation of the product's expected performance helps. If the cardholder's "defect" complaint conflicts with the product's stated performance specifications, cite those specifications in your rebuttal. However, if the cardholder can show the item failed to perform any core function, the defect claim is usually valid.

**Q: The customer returned the item, but I believe it was deliberately damaged before return. Can I fight the 13.3 after receiving the return?**
A: This is a challenging situation. If you can document (with photos taken immediately upon receiving the return) that the item was returned in a condition that does not match reasonable normal use, you may have an argument. However, 13.3 chargebacks after successful returns are uncommon — focus energy here on proving the condition of the return versus the customer's claim, and consult your acquirer about the specific timing rules.

**Q: My return policy is "no returns." Does that protect me from 13.3 chargebacks?**
A: No. Visa's rules override a merchant's no-return policy for genuine defect and not-as-described disputes. A cardholder who receives a defective or misdescribed item is entitled to a chargeback remedy regardless of the merchant's stated return policy. However, a no-return policy that was clearly disclosed does limit some types of 13.3 disputes — particularly where the cardholder simply changed their mind.

**Q: The cardholder claims the item was "cheap quality" but my listing never claimed it was premium. Is this a valid 13.3?**
A: Not necessarily. If your product listing accurately described the materials, specifications, and origin of the product, and the cardholder's complaint is that the item felt cheap rather than that it differed from the description, the 13.3 may not have merit. Submit your original listing showing the accurate description. Quality disputes where the description was honest but the cardholder expected more are generally winnable.

**Q: How do I prevent 13.3 chargebacks?**
A: The most effective prevention measures are: (1) accurate, detailed product descriptions with dimensions, materials, and photos from multiple angles; (2) quality control before shipment; (3) responsive customer service that resolves quality complaints with a replacement or refund before a chargeback is filed; and (4) a clear, easy return process. Customers who can easily get a resolution from you directly rarely file chargebacks.
