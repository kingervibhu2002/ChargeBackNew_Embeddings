# Visa 13.5 — Misrepresentation

Visa reason code 13.5 is filed when a cardholder claims the merchant misrepresented the goods, services, or terms of the transaction — either through false advertising, hidden fees, material omissions, or terms that changed without the cardholder's knowledge. This code goes beyond quality (13.3) and quantity (13.1) disputes: it challenges the truthfulness of the merchant's representation at the time of sale. Misrepresentation disputes are common in travel, subscription services, and any industry where pricing or terms involve complexity that is not fully disclosed upfront.

## What This Chargeback Means

A 13.5 chargeback is the cardholder asserting: "What I was told or shown when I agreed to pay is materially different from what was actually delivered or charged."

This covers a wide range of scenarios:

**False Advertising:** A merchant's marketing claimed the product had features or capabilities it does not actually have. The cardholder purchased based on those claims.

**Hidden or Undisclosed Fees:** The cardholder was shown one price and charged an additional amount not disclosed at checkout — resort fees, processing fees, service charges, or add-ons added without clear disclosure.

**Auto-Renewal Not Disclosed:** The cardholder signed up for what they believed was a one-time purchase or a free trial, but was not clearly informed that it would auto-renew as a paid subscription.

**Terms Changed After Purchase:** The merchant altered material terms of the agreement (pricing, service level, scope) after the cardholder completed the transaction, without explicit consent.

**Material Omission:** The merchant failed to disclose information that would have been material to the cardholder's purchase decision — a known defect, a restriction, a condition.

## High-Risk Industries for 13.5 Chargebacks

### Travel and Hospitality
Hotels that charge resort fees not disclosed in the room rate shown at booking are among the most common sources of 13.5 chargebacks. Cardholders book a room at the displayed rate, check out, and discover they were charged an additional $30–$50 per night "resort fee" that was buried in fine print or not shown until check-in.

**Required disclosure:** Resort fees must be prominently disclosed in the displayed room rate or clearly shown as a mandatory additional charge before the cardholder completes the booking.

### Subscription and SaaS
Hidden auto-renewal terms are a major source of 13.5 chargebacks. A cardholder signs up for a "30-day free trial" without clearly understanding they will be charged automatically at the end of the trial period.

**Required disclosure:** Auto-renewal terms, including the billing amount and frequency, must be explicitly disclosed at sign-up — not buried in terms of service that the customer clicks through.

### Retail and E-Commerce
Products advertised with specific features that the delivered item does not have. "Waterproof" items that leak, "organic" products that are not certified, "includes free shipping" where hidden handling charges are added.

## Evidence You Need to Win a 13.5

The central question in every 13.5 dispute is: what exactly did the merchant represent, and when? Your evidence must demonstrate that your representations were accurate and that all material terms were clearly disclosed.

### Website Screenshots (Timestamped)

Archived screenshots of your website — product pages, pricing pages, terms and conditions pages — as they appeared at the time of the cardholder's purchase are your most important evidence.

**Using the Wayback Machine:** The Internet Archive's Wayback Machine (web.archive.org) archives periodic snapshots of public websites. If your page was indexed, you can retrieve its content as it appeared near the purchase date. Submit the Wayback Machine URL and the screenshot as evidence.

**Internal page archives:** Better than the Wayback Machine is maintaining your own internal archive of page screenshots every time material content changes. Store these systematically with timestamps.

**Important:** Screenshots taken today of how your page looks today are far weaker than screenshots demonstrating how the page looked when the cardholder purchased. A merchant who can demonstrate consistent, accurate disclosure over time is in a strong position.

### Terms at Checkout

If your checkout flow displays terms of service, auto-renewal disclosures, fee schedules, or pricing breakdowns, submit screenshots of each checkout step showing:
- The total amount displayed before payment confirmation
- Any additional fees clearly itemized
- Auto-renewal language if applicable
- A "confirm order" or acknowledgment step where the cardholder actively accepted the terms

### Disclosure of Specific Terms

For resort fees, hidden charges, or subscription terms: submit the specific disclosure as it appeared in the booking flow, order summary, or sign-up process. If the fee was disclosed, even in a sub-bullet on a pricing page, showing that disclosure is your defense.

**Best practice:** Disclosures should be proximate — near the pricing information, not buried in a 40-page terms document that no one reads. The closer the disclosure is to the payment action, the stronger the argument that the cardholder was meaningfully informed.

### Email Confirmations

The confirmation email sent to the cardholder at purchase, clearly showing all charges, fees, and subscription terms. This is timestamped, customer-specific evidence of what was disclosed at the transaction moment.

## When Merchants Win 13.5 Disputes

Merchants typically win when:

- Clear, prominent, pre-purchase disclosure of all fees and terms can be demonstrated
- The cardholder's claim that a term was "hidden" is refuted by showing it was displayed at checkout
- Product capabilities match what was stated in the listing (accurate specifications, truthful feature claims)
- Subscription auto-renewal was disclosed in a conspicuous, pre-payment acknowledgment

## When Merchants Lose 13.5 Disputes

Merchants typically lose when:

- Fees were not disclosed before the cardholder completed payment (resort fees revealed only at check-in, shipping fees added after checkout begins, etc.)
- Auto-renewal was buried in fine-print terms not specifically called out at sign-up
- Advertised features were materially inaccurate
- The merchant altered terms after the purchase date without the cardholder's explicit consent

## Regulatory Context: FTC and State Requirements

Misrepresentation in advertising and disclosure failures are not just chargeback risks — they are potential regulatory violations under the Federal Trade Commission Act, state consumer protection laws, and the FTC's Negative Option Rule (for subscription products).

Merchants who fail to make clear and conspicuous disclosures of material terms face FTC enforcement risk in addition to chargeback exposure. This is why disclosure compliance is both a consumer law obligation and a chargeback prevention strategy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: A cardholder claims I advertised a feature my product doesn't have. My website is accurate now, but I'm not sure what it said when they purchased. What do I do?**
A: This is a difficult situation if you cannot reconstruct the page as it appeared at purchase. Check the Wayback Machine for any archived versions near the transaction date. If no archive exists and you cannot confirm the page was accurate, assess honestly whether the cardholder's claim could be valid. If your page did have inaccurate claims at any point, accept this chargeback and correct the listing immediately.

**Q: I disclosed the resort fee in my terms and conditions. Is that sufficient?**
A: Courts and card networks have increasingly moved toward the standard that disclosures must be "clear and conspicuous" — meaning in a location and in language that a reasonable person would actually read and understand. A resort fee buried in a 30-page terms document that users click through without reading is unlikely to be considered adequate disclosure. The fee should be visible in the booking flow, near the pricing information, before the payment confirmation step.

**Q: A customer says my free trial auto-renewal wasn't disclosed. My sign-up page clearly states it. How do I prove this?**
A: Submit a screenshot of your sign-up page showing the auto-renewal disclosure as it appeared at the time of sign-up. If you can also show that the cardholder clicked through a specific acknowledgment (a checked box saying "I understand my subscription will auto-renew at $X per month") at enrollment, that is even stronger. The enrollment confirmation email, if it restated the renewal terms, is additional supporting evidence.

**Q: Travel merchant: a cardholder booked through an OTA (Online Travel Agency) and disputes the resort fee. Am I liable?**
A: This depends on the booking flow. If the OTA did not disclose the resort fee in the displayed rate, the liability question may involve both you and the OTA. For chargebacks, the merchant of record is responsible. Ensure your OTA agreements require them to disclose all mandatory fees in the displayed rate. For chargebacks resulting from OTA disclosure failures, work with your acquirer on the representment and consider your contractual claims against the OTA.

**Q: Can I include a mandatory arbitration clause or class action waiver to protect against misrepresentation chargebacks?**
A: Contractual arbitration clauses govern legal disputes between parties — they do not eliminate chargeback rights, which are governed by card network rules, not contract law. A cardholder retains their right to file a chargeback through their issuer regardless of what your terms and conditions say about dispute resolution. Arbitration clauses are relevant to civil litigation, not the chargeback process.
