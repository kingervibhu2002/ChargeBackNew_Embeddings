# Visa 13.4 — Counterfeit Merchandise

Visa reason code 13.4 is filed when a cardholder claims the item they received is counterfeit — a fake, unauthorized copy, or imitation of a genuine branded product. This is among the most difficult chargeback reason codes for merchants to defend because the determination of authenticity involves third-party expert judgment, and card networks and issuers tend to strongly favor cardholder protection in counterfeiting allegations. Understanding the limitations of your defense options — and the significant implications of operating in categories susceptible to this code — is essential.

## What This Chargeback Means

A 13.4 chargeback is the cardholder asserting: "The branded product I received is not genuine. It is a counterfeit or unauthorized copy of the authentic product."

This is distinct from 13.3 (not as described), where the issue is the merchant's own description being inaccurate. In a 13.4, the cardholder is claiming the product misrepresents itself as something it is not — specifically, as a genuine branded item (designer goods, licensed merchandise, authenticated collectibles, branded electronics, pharmaceuticals) when it is actually a fake.

## Why This Chargeback Is Hard to Fight

### The Authenticity Standard Is Expert-Determined

Proving that an item is genuine — not counterfeit — often requires documentation from the original brand or manufacturer. Cards networks and issuing banks do not have the expertise to inspect physical items and make authenticity determinations themselves. They rely on documentation and trust the cardholder's claim, especially when the cardholder provides a statement from an expert or authorized dealer saying the item is fake.

### Issuers Strongly Favor Cardholders on Counterfeit Claims

Counterfeit merchandise creates legal and reputational risk for card networks — being known as a network that facilitates counterfeit goods purchases is damaging. This makes issuers predisposed to accept 13.4 claims and issue the chargeback without extensive scrutiny of the merchant's position.

### The Merchant Cannot Inspect the Returned Item at the Time of the Dispute

In a chargeback process, you are responding to paperwork — you do not have the item in hand to provide to an authenticator while the dispute is pending. By the time a 13.4 chargeback arrives, the cardholder still has the item (or claims to) and you cannot independently verify whether their counterfeit allegation is accurate.

## Evidence for Defending a 13.4 Chargeback

Despite the difficulty, merchants do have defenses available if the merchandise they sell is genuinely authentic.

### Authenticity Certificates and Documentation
For branded goods, obtain and retain certificates of authenticity provided by the manufacturer or authorized distributor. Submit:
- Certificate of authenticity (COA) for the specific item or batch
- Invoice from your authorized distributor showing the items were purchased from a legitimate supply chain
- Any brand authorization letter confirming your status as an authorized reseller

### Supplier and Manufacturer Records
Your supply chain documentation is critical:
- Purchase invoices from your supplier showing the product name, brand, quantity, and price paid (authentic branded items have cost structures that differ significantly from counterfeit goods)
- Supplier contact information confirming they are an authorized distributor for the brand
- Any quality certificates or import documents showing the merchandise entered the country through legitimate channels

### Product Serial Numbers
Many authentic branded products include unique serial numbers that can be verified through the manufacturer's database. If your items have serial numbers:
- Record serial numbers at the time of purchase from your supplier
- Include serial numbers in your shipment records
- The serial number verification provides an objective, brand-supported authenticity confirmation

### Your Authorized Reseller Status
If you are an authorized reseller of the brand being claimed as counterfeit, documentation of your authorized reseller agreement is powerful evidence. An authorized reseller, by definition, sources authentic goods from the brand's supply chain.

## When to Accept the Chargeback

Accept the 13.4 chargeback without fighting it when:

- You cannot produce supply chain documentation demonstrating legitimate sourcing
- Your supplier's authorization status is unclear or their documentation is insufficient
- The item's price point and margin suggest it was sourced outside the authorized supply chain
- You have received multiple 13.4 chargebacks on similar products (a pattern suggesting a genuine sourcing problem)

Attempting to fight a 13.4 without strong authenticity documentation is unlikely to succeed and wastes resources. More importantly, if the items you are selling are genuinely counterfeit (whether knowingly or through negligent sourcing), continuing to fight chargebacks while selling those items creates legal liability beyond the financial dispute.

## Brand Protection Implications

A pattern of 13.4 chargebacks against a merchant can trigger action beyond the chargeback dispute system:

**Brand owner enforcement:** Brand owners monitor online marketplaces and payment processors for counterfeiting. A merchant generating 13.4 chargebacks may attract brand enforcement action — takedown notices, cease-and-desist letters, or civil litigation.

**Acquirer review:** An acquirer receiving multiple 13.4 chargebacks on a merchant account will investigate the merchant's product sourcing. They may require documentation of authorized distributor relationships or terminate the account if they cannot confirm legitimate sourcing.

**MATCH listing risk:** If your account is terminated as a result of counterfeit-related chargebacks, you may be placed on the MATCH list (Terminated Merchant File), making it very difficult to obtain processing through another acquirer.

## Anti-Counterfeiting Measures That Help Prevention

### Source Only From Authorized Distributors
This is the most important prevention measure. Verify that every brand you carry is sourced from the brand's authorized distribution network. A price that seems too good to be true from an unfamiliar supplier is often a signal of counterfeit sourcing.

### Obtain Authorization Letters Before Carrying Brands
Many brands will issue authorization letters to legitimate resellers confirming their authorized status. Obtain these before listing brand-name products and keep them on file for chargeback defense.

### Product Authentication Technology
For high-value collectibles, luxury goods, or branded electronics, implement product authentication at intake — compare received items against known-authentic samples, use UV verification marks, RFID tags, or manufacturer-verified serial numbers.

### Display Authenticity Documentation on Your Product Listings
For high-value branded goods, display your authorized reseller status and certificate of authenticity information on your product listing page. Cardholders who see this documentation are less likely to suspect counterfeit and less likely to file a 13.4 chargeback.

### Accept Returns Readily for Authenticity Concerns
A merchant who accepts returns with full refunds for cardholder authenticity concerns — without requiring the cardholder to file a chargeback — reduces the chargeback impact. If your product is genuine, a return and refund preserves the customer relationship and avoids the chargeback. If your product is actually counterfeit, issuing refunds and investigating your supply chain is the right course of action.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: A cardholder says my product is fake, but I bought it from a legitimate source. How do I prove it?**
A: Submit your full supply chain documentation: purchase invoice from your supplier, your supplier's authorized distributor agreement with the brand (or their letter confirming authorized status), and any certificate of authenticity provided with the goods. If the brand manufacturer can confirm the serial number is genuine, include that confirmation. The combination of legitimate sourcing records and serial number authentication is your strongest possible defense.

**Q: Can I fight a 13.4 if I do not sell branded goods — the item is my own brand?**
A: A 13.4 chargeback filed on your own branded (non-licensed, non-third-party branded) products is likely misapplied. Your own brand products cannot be "counterfeit" in the traditional sense — they are whatever your brand says they are. In your representment, clarify that the product is your proprietary brand, not a copy of a third-party brand, and provide your product documentation. This argument typically succeeds when clearly presented.

**Q: I am on a marketplace (Amazon, eBay). Am I responsible for 13.4 chargebacks on marketplace sales?**
A: On marketplaces that process payments themselves (like Amazon's platform), chargebacks are typically handled by the marketplace, not directly by the merchant. However, counterfeit complaints on marketplace platforms have serious consequences — account suspension, legal action by brand owners, and loss of selling privileges. Whether payment is handled through the marketplace or directly, ensuring authentic sourcing is essential.

**Q: What do I do if I discover my supplier was selling counterfeit goods?**
A: Stop selling those products immediately. Notify your acquirer proactively. Accept any outstanding 13.4 chargebacks. Issue refunds to customers who received those goods. Consult legal counsel about your liability to the brand owners and to customers. Proactive disclosure to your acquirer is far better than having the situation discovered through escalating chargebacks and account review.

**Q: Does a high percentage of 13.4 chargebacks affect my processing relationship?**
A: Yes, significantly. Multiple 13.4 chargebacks signal to your acquirer and card networks that you may be selling counterfeit goods. This triggers account review, potentially requiring you to demonstrate legitimate sourcing for every product line. Repeated 13.4 chargebacks without a credible defense can result in account termination and MATCH listing.
