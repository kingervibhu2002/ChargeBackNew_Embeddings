# Second Chargeback (Pre-Arbitration): What It Is and What to Do

You submitted your evidence, wrote a solid rebuttal letter, and waited — only to receive another notice stating that the issuer is maintaining the dispute. This is the second chargeback, formally called a pre-arbitration filing. It is the issuer's way of saying your evidence was either insufficient, disputed by the cardholder, or procedurally defective. Understanding what happens at this stage, what your options are, and how to decide whether escalation makes financial sense is critical before you spend money on arbitration fees.

## What Is a Second Chargeback?

A second chargeback (also called a pre-arbitration or pre-arb) occurs when an issuing bank reviews your representment response and determines that the dispute is not resolved. The issuer re-files the dispute against your acquirer, which in turn notifies you that the chargeback has been re-asserted.

This is the second and final opportunity for the parties to resolve the dispute before it escalates to formal network arbitration — where the card network itself (Visa or Mastercard) acts as judge and issues a binding decision.

Pre-arbitration is not automatic. An issuer files pre-arb only when they believe their cardholder's claim still stands after reviewing your evidence. If your representment was clearly decisive and the issuer silently accepts it (you see the funds return to your account), no pre-arb occurs.

## Why Issuers File Pre-Arbitration

Issuers file pre-arbitration for several reasons:

- **Your evidence was insufficient:** The representment did not conclusively counter the cardholder's specific claim. For example, you submitted tracking data but the cardholder claims the package arrived damaged — and you did not address that.
- **New information from the cardholder:** After seeing your evidence, the cardholder provided additional rebuttal information to the issuer that undermines your representment.
- **Procedural defect in your submission:** Your representment missed a required field, was submitted late (even one day past deadline), or used an incorrect format under the network's rules.
- **The issuer disagrees with your interpretation:** In consumer dispute codes, issuers frequently side with cardholders on subjective questions (was the product as described? was the service satisfactory?).

## Timing: When to Expect It

Pre-arbitration typically arrives **15–30 days after your representment was submitted**. The exact window depends on the card network:

- **Visa:** The issuer has 30 days from the date your representment was processed to file pre-arbitration.
- **Mastercard:** The issuer generally has 45 days from your second presentment to file pre-arbitration.

If no pre-arb is filed within these windows, your representment is considered accepted and the case is closed in your favor.

Once you receive the pre-arb notice, your response window is typically **10–30 days** depending on the network and acquirer. Missing this deadline means the pre-arb is automatically accepted and the funds are debited from your account — no further recourse available.

## Your Two Options

When you receive a pre-arbitration notice, you face a binary choice:

### Option 1: Accept the Loss

You agree with the issuer's reassertion of the dispute and absorb the chargeback amount. The funds are debited from your account and the case is closed. No arbitration fees are incurred.

**When this is the right choice:**
- Your evidence was genuinely insufficient to counter the cardholder's claim
- The transaction amount does not justify the cost of arbitration
- The issuer filed pre-arb on procedural grounds and correcting the defect would not change the outcome
- The cardholder has provided new information that you cannot credibly counter

### Option 2: Escalate to Arbitration

You formally contest the pre-arb by escalating to card network arbitration. The network reviews all documentation from both sides and issues a binding ruling. The losing party pays all arbitration fees.

**When this may be the right choice:**
- You have strong, unambiguous evidence that directly addresses the cardholder's claim
- The transaction amount is large enough to justify the arbitration fees and risk
- The issuer's pre-arb appears to be procedurally improper (wrong reason code, missed their own deadline, etc.)
- You have documented evidence of repeat fraudulent behavior by this cardholder

## Cost Analysis: The Arbitration Fee Risk

Arbitration is expensive. Understanding the full cost exposure before escalating is essential.

**Visa Arbitration Fees:**
- Filing fee: $250 per case (paid by the merchant to the network to escalate)
- If merchant loses: Network may award the filing fee to the issuer as well, meaning total merchant exposure = transaction amount + $250–$500

**Mastercard Arbitration Fees:**
- Filing fee: $250–$500 per case
- Administrative fees may apply depending on case complexity
- Loser-pays principle applies: if the issuer loses, they pay; if you lose, you pay

**Total Worst-Case Exposure:**
```
Worst case = Transaction amount + Arbitration filing fee + Issuer's legal/admin fees
```

For a $200 transaction, your worst-case loss at arbitration could be $200 + $500 = $700 — more than three times the original dispute amount.

**Break-Even Analysis:**

Before escalating, calculate the expected value of arbitration:

```
Expected Value = (Win Probability × Transaction Amount) − (Loss Probability × (Transaction Amount + Arbitration Fees))
```

If win probability is 40% on a $300 transaction with $500 in fees:
- Expected recovery: 0.40 × $300 = $120
- Expected loss: 0.60 × ($300 + $500) = $480
- Net expected value: $120 − $480 = **−$360 — Do not escalate**

Arbitration makes financial sense only when your win probability is high AND the transaction amount significantly exceeds the fee exposure.

## When to Escalate: Decision Framework

### Strong Escalation Signals
- Transaction amount exceeds $1,000
- You have 3DS authentication data proving the cardholder authorized the transaction
- You have delivery confirmation with GPS coordinates and photo-at-door evidence
- The same cardholder has filed multiple disputes against your business (documented friendly fraud)
- The issuer appears to have filed pre-arb in violation of network rules (e.g., after their deadline)
- Your legal or compliance team believes the issuer's position is procedurally improper

### Strong Accept Signals
- Transaction amount is under $250 (fees likely exceed recovery)
- Your initial representment was factually weak or based on incomplete evidence
- The cardholder has provided new, credible information you cannot rebut
- The dispute involves a subjective quality claim (product "not as described") where the issuer will side with the cardholder
- You cannot locate the key evidence needed to strengthen your position

## A Note on Compliance as an Alternative

If the issuer filed the pre-arb in violation of card network rules — for example, using the wrong reason code, missing their own deadline, or re-filing a dispute that was already resolved — you may have grounds to file a **compliance case** rather than standard arbitration. Compliance cases challenge procedural violations and can be less expensive than full arbitration. Consult your acquirer before assuming arbitration is your only escalation path.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is a second chargeback the same as arbitration?**
A: No. Pre-arbitration is the issuer re-asserting the dispute after your representment. Arbitration is the next step — where the card network itself decides the outcome. Pre-arb is the last chance to resolve the dispute without paying arbitration fees. Many cases are settled (merchants accept the loss) at the pre-arb stage rather than escalating.

**Q: Can I submit new evidence at the pre-arbitration stage?**
A: Yes, and you should. If the issuer filed pre-arb because your original evidence was insufficient, the pre-arb response is your opportunity to address whatever gap existed. Submitting the same evidence you already submitted is unlikely to change the outcome. Identify what the issuer's specific objection was and address it directly.

**Q: How often do merchants win at arbitration?**
A: Win rates at arbitration are lower than at first representment — typically 30–45% for merchants, compared to 40–60% at initial representment. Card networks have historically been cardholder-friendly in their arbitration decisions. This is one reason the cost-benefit analysis must be rigorous before escalating.

**Q: My acquirer is advising me to accept the loss. Should I follow their advice?**
A: Acquirers have their own risk considerations and may advise acceptance to avoid the administrative burden of arbitration on their end. However, the financial decision is yours. If you believe you have strong evidence and the amount justifies the fee risk, you can push back and request escalation. Get the analysis in writing.

**Q: What happens if I miss the pre-arb response deadline?**
A: Missing the pre-arb deadline is treated as automatic acceptance of the dispute. The funds are debited from your account and you have no further recourse — even if your underlying evidence is strong. Deadlines at this stage are absolute.
