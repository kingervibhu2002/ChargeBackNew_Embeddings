# How to Write a Winning Chargeback Rebuttal Letter

A chargeback rebuttal letter is your formal written argument to the issuing bank explaining why the disputed transaction was valid and should be decided in your favor. The letter accompanies your evidence package and is often the first thing a chargeback analyst reads. A poorly structured or emotional letter can cost you a dispute even when your underlying evidence is strong. This guide covers every component of a professional, persuasive rebuttal.

---

## Why the Rebuttal Letter Matters

Card network rules (Visa VCR, Mastercard Dispute Resolution) allow merchants to submit a rebuttal—called a representment—to challenge a chargeback. Analysts at the issuing bank typically spend 2–5 minutes reviewing a complete response package. A clear, concise letter directs their attention to the strongest evidence, makes the analyst's job easier, and increases your win rate. Without a letter, your evidence bundle looks like a pile of documents with no narrative.

---

## Structure of a Winning Rebuttal Letter

### Opening Paragraph

State who you are, what you are disputing, and your position in one clear sentence. Reference the chargeback case number, the card network reason code, and the transaction date immediately.

**Example:**
> "We write in response to Chargeback Case [CASE NUMBER], initiated under Visa Reason Code 10.4 (Card Absent Environment – Other Fraud), for a transaction of [AMOUNT] processed on [DATE]. We respectfully dispute this chargeback and submit the following evidence demonstrating that the transaction was authorized and fulfilled."

Keep the opening to three sentences or fewer. Do not begin with pleasantries.

### Transaction Summary Section

Provide a factual summary of the transaction in 4–6 bullet points:

- Transaction date and time
- Transaction amount and currency
- Card number (last four digits only)
- Authorization code
- AVS and CVV response codes
- Order or invoice number

This section gives the analyst an at-a-glance snapshot without having to hunt through attachments. Label it **"Transaction Details"** or **"Transaction Summary."**

### Reason Code Response Section

Address the specific claim being made by the cardholder. Each reason code has a defined dispute category—fraud, not received, not as described, cancelled recurring, etc. Directly rebut the claim using specific facts:

- If the dispute is fraud: explain what authentication data proves the card was present or the genuine cardholder initiated the transaction.
- If the dispute is non-delivery: cite the carrier tracking number, delivery timestamp, and confirmed address.
- If the dispute is not as described: compare the cardholder's claim against the original product description with exhibit references.

This section is the heart of your letter. Do not make general statements ("our products are high quality"). Make specific, verifiable claims ("the item was delivered to 123 Main Street on [DATE] at 2:14 PM per UPS Tracking #1Z999").

### Evidence List

After your narrative, include a numbered list of all attached exhibits:

```
Exhibit 1: Authorization Record (AVS = Y, CVV = M)
Exhibit 2: Carrier Tracking – Delivered [DATE]
Exhibit 3: Signed Delivery Confirmation
Exhibit 4: Customer IP Address and Device Fingerprint at Checkout
Exhibit 5: Cardholder's Shipping Address Confirmation Email
```

Number exhibits sequentially and label each physical document with the matching exhibit number in the top-right corner. Analysts refer to exhibits while reading your letter; if labels don't match, they lose trust in your submission.

### Closing Paragraph

Restate your position, explicitly ask the issuing bank to reverse the chargeback and restore funds, and reference the total disputed amount. Keep this to two sentences.

**Example:**
> "Based on the evidence above, the transaction of [AMOUNT] was authorized by the legitimate cardholder, fulfilled as ordered, and delivered to the confirmed address. We respectfully request that this chargeback be reversed and the funds of [AMOUNT] be restored to our account."

Include your name, title, contact email, and merchant ID at the bottom.

---

## Tone and Style

- **Professional and factual.** Never express frustration, accuse the cardholder of lying directly, or use emotional language.
- **Active voice.** "We delivered the product" is stronger than "the product was delivered."
- **Short sentences.** Analysts skim under time pressure. Avoid paragraphs longer than four sentences.
- **No jargon** beyond standard chargeback terminology. Do not reference internal system names an issuer cannot verify.
- **No threats.** Do not reference legal action, regulatory complaints, or media. It looks defensive and wastes space.

---

## Length

One to two pages maximum. If your letter exceeds two pages, your narrative is too long and you risk burying the key arguments. Move detailed data into exhibits. The letter should tell the story; the exhibits should prove it.

---

## Labeling Exhibits

Every attached document must be labeled **before** submission:

1. Print or export each document.
2. Write or stamp "Exhibit [NUMBER]" in the top-right corner.
3. If submitting digitally, add a text overlay or rename the file: `Exhibit_1_Authorization_Record.pdf`
4. Create a cover sheet that lists all exhibits in order.

Mislabeled or unlabeled exhibits are one of the top reasons issuers cannot validate a response and rule against the merchant.

---

## Submission Methods

- **Acquirer portal:** Most acquirers provide a dispute management portal. Upload your PDF package there. Keep the confirmation email as proof of submission.
- **Fax:** Some issuers still accept fax. Get a confirmation report.
- **Mail:** Use certified mail if required. Retain the tracking number.
- **Always confirm receipt.** Submit early enough to allow for re-submission if the initial attempt fails.

---

## What Issuers Look For

Issuing bank analysts are not card network staff. They follow internal procedures and look for:

1. **A matching reason code response.** Does your rebuttal actually address the dispute category?
2. **Authenticated evidence.** Documents that can be traced back to a verifiable source (carrier website, payment processor record, CRM export).
3. **Consistency.** Does the address in the shipping record match the billing address on the card?
4. **Timeline coherence.** Do dates and times across all exhibits form a logical sequence?

---

## Common Mistakes That Lose Disputes

- Submitting after the deadline (most Visa deadlines are 30 days; Mastercard 45 days from the chargeback date).
- Responding to the wrong reason code (always check the code on the chargeback notice, not the description).
- Including irrelevant evidence that doesn't address the dispute claim.
- Sending a form letter without any transaction-specific information.
- Omitting a letter entirely and submitting only documents.
- Referencing a tracking number that shows the package is still in transit.
- Failing to include the authorization code.

---

## Timing

File your response as early as possible within the window. Submitting on day 28 of a 30-day window leaves no buffer for technical issues. Aim for day 10–15. Some acquirers have internal deadlines that are shorter than the card network deadline—always check your acquirer's specific cutoff.
